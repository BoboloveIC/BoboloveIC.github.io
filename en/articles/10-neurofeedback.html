Neurofeedback: Train Your Brain With Your Own Signals

Published: 2026-06-03 Tags: NeuroscienceSelf-Growth 8 min read

Imagine if you could see your brain activity in real time — and learn to change it, just like you learn to play a musical instrument. That is the promise of neurofeedback.

Also called EEG biofeedback, neurofeedback is a type of brain training that gives you instant visual or auditory feedback about your brain waves, allowing you to learn (through trial and error) how to produce healthier brain patterns.

How it works (simplified): Sensors on your scalp measure your EEG. A computer translates your brain waves into a game-like display. When your brain produces the "target" wave pattern, you get a reward (a video plays, a tone sounds, points go up). Your brain naturally learns to reproduce the rewarded pattern — that is neuroplasticity in action.

A Brief History

Neurofeedback is not new. It was discovered accidentally in the 1960s by Joe Kamiya at the University of Chicago. He found that people could learn to produce Alpha waves on command if given real-time feedback — the birth of "Alpha training."

In the 1970s, Barry Sterman discovered that neurofeedback could reduce seizures in cats — and later in humans. This opened the door to neurofeedback for epilepsy.

Today, neurofeedback is used for ADHD, anxiety, depression, PTSD, insomnia, epilepsy, and peak performance training (athletes, executives).

Types of Neurofeedback

1. Frequency Band Training (Traditional)

This is the most common type. It targets specific EEG frequency bands:

  • Increase Alpha (8-13 Hz): For anxiety, stress, and relaxation training
  • Decrease Theta/Beta ratio: For ADHD (increasing Beta for focus, decreasing excessive Theta)
  • Increase SMR (12-15 Hz): For ADHD and improving focused attention

2. Infra-Low Frequency (ILF) Neurofeedback

ILF targets very slow brain waves (0.1 Hz or less). It is often used for complex trauma, migraines, and autonomic regulation. ILF is more individualized than frequency band training.

3. LORETA Neurofeedback

LORETA (Low Resolution Electromagnetic Tomography) targets deep brain structures (not just the cortex under the scalp). It is used for more complex conditions like depression, PTSD, and traumatic brain injury.

What Does the Evidence Say?

Neurofeedback has more than 50 years of research, but the quality of evidence varies by condition.

ADHD: Strongest Evidence

The evidence for ADHD is the strongest. A 2014 meta-analysis (Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews) of 15 RCTs found a large effect size (d = 0.8) for inattention and impulsivity — comparable to stimulant medication.

Important: The American Academy of Pediatrics gave neurofeedback a "Level 1 — Best Support" rating for ADHD treatment (same level as stimulant medication).

ADHD & Neurofeedback — Key Finding: Unlike medication, the benefits of neurofeedback appear to be lasting. Multiple follow-up studies show that gains are maintained 6-12 months after training ends (because the brain has learned a new skill, not just responding to a chemical).

Anxiety and Depression: Moderate Evidence

Several RCTs show that Alpha/Theta neurofeedback reduces anxiety and depression symptoms, with effect sizes in the medium range (d = 0.4-0.6). It is generally used as a complement to therapy or medication, not a replacement.

Epilepsy: Well-Established

Neurofeedback for epilepsy has strong evidence — it is even covered by some insurance in Europe. Studies show a 30-60% reduction in seizure frequency after 20-40 sessions.

PTSD: Emerging Evidence

Early studies (mostly small RCTs) suggest that neurofeedback (especially Alpha/Theta and ILF) can reduce PTSD symptoms, including flashbacks and hyperarousal. Larger studies are underway.

What Happens in a Neurofeedback Session?

  1. Assessment (Session 1-2): QEEG (quantitative EEG) "brain map" to identify which areas need training
  2. Placement: 1-4 sensors placed on specific scalp locations (painless, no electricity enters your brain)
  3. The "Game": You watch a video or play a simple game. When your brain produces the target pattern, the video plays clearly / the car moves faster. When you drift, the video gets fuzzy / the car slows down
  4. Duration: 30-45 minutes per session, 1-3 times per week
  5. Course length: Typically 20-40 sessions for lasting results

Consumer Devices: Can You Do It at Home?

Several consumer EEG headsets now offer "neurofeedback-like" experiences:

  • Muse: Uses "audio feedback" (rain sounds when calm, storm when distracted) — not true neurofeedback, but a good introduction
  • Neurosity Crown: Tracks focus and relaxation states, offers some training games
  • BrainCo Focus: A headband specifically marketed for ADHD training (still in early research stage)

Caveat: Consumer devices are not a substitute for clinical neurofeedback with a trained professional. They can be useful for wellness and self-awareness, but they lack the precision and individualization of clinical-grade systems.

Is Neurofeedback Safe?

Yes — it is non-invasive and drug-free. No electrical current enters the brain; it is purely measurement and feedback.

Possible (rare) side effects include:temporary headache, fatigue, or "over-arousal" (feeling jittery after a session). These are usually mild and resolve quickly. A skilled practitioner adjusts the protocol to avoid over-training.

Finding a provider: In the US, look for practitioners certified by ISNR (International Society for Neuroregulation & Research) or BCIA (Biofeedback Certification International Alliance). These certifications ensure proper training and ethical standards.

Neurofeedback vs. Meditation: Which Should You Choose?

Dimension Neurofeedback Meditation
Cost High ($100-200/session) Free / low cost
Evidence strength Strong for ADHD/epilepsy Strong for stress/anxiety/depression
Accessibility Requires trained provider Can start today, anywhere
Time to effect 10-20 sessions Days to weeks

Bottom line: Meditation is the best first step for most people. Neurofeedback is worth considering if: (1) you have a specific condition like ADHD or epilepsy; (2) meditation alone has not been sufficient; (3) you have access to a qualified provider.

Conclusion: Neurofeedback is not magic, but it is a powerful tool that leverages your brain's own plasticity. With 50+ years of research and growing clinical acceptance, it offers a drug-free option for training your brain. Whether you try clinical neurofeedback or a consumer device, the core insight is empowering: your brain can learn. And what can be learned can be changed.
